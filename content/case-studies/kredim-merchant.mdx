## Problem

A merchant who wanted to offer Kredim credit to their own customers had no
surface of their own to do it from. The credit product existed and the customer
was standing at the counter, but the person serving them had no way to complete
the sale directly.

The constraint that shapes everything else is who the user is. This is store
staff, mid-sale, with a customer waiting in front of them. That is not an
audience for a dense administrative interface. Whatever the panel does, it has
to be quick, hard to get wrong, and tolerant of a shop's connectivity.

## Architecture

Kredim Merchant is a partner-facing panel on the same platform as the Member
Panel: React 18, Redux Toolkit slices, and Redux-Saga for the async sequences.
The credit, campaign, and authentication logic already lived there. What Merchant
adds is a different tenant with a different shape of permission on top of it.

<MerchantTenant />

Visibility is scoped by branch, not only by merchant. A merchant organisation
contains branches, and branches contain the staff who actually use the panel. A
head office can see across its branches; a single store sees its own trade. That
is one level deeper than the Member Panel's model, where the tenant boundary was
the whole story.

The product surface is deliberately narrow. The panel's job is the transaction
at the counter: extending credit to a customer for a purchase, and reversing it
when a sale is cancelled or refunded. Reporting and settlement live elsewhere.

## Decisions

**Scope by branch at the data, not at the screen.** A branch scope is a
visibility rule about data, so it belongs where data is fetched rather than
where it is rendered. Enforcing it by hiding rows in the interface would mean
every new screen is a fresh opportunity to leak another branch's trade, and the
one that forgets is the one nobody notices. Deciding it at the boundary makes
the safe case the default and the unsafe one something you would have to work at.

**Money has to add up, and the interface is the weak point.** Every transaction
the panel creates has a financial consequence that must reconcile later, even
though the reconciliation itself happens elsewhere. A sale, a cancellation, a
refund, and a transaction that failed halfway all have to leave the system in a
state that adds up.

That puts the correctness burden somewhere awkward. The interface is a simple
one, used at speed, by people whose job is serving a customer rather than
keeping books, and the records it produces have to be exactly right. The design
answer is to make the ambiguous states unreachable instead of recoverable: a
transaction is completed or it is not, a reversal refers to the specific sale it
reverses, and there is no half-finished state a cashier can walk away from for
somebody to interpret later.

**Reuse the platform, not the interface.** Merchant shares the Member Panel's
state architecture and its credit logic, but none of its screens. A member
managing their own credit and a cashier completing a sale want opposite things:
one is browsing, the other is executing a single task as fast as it can be done.
Sharing the platform kept the money logic in one place. Sharing the interface
would have produced a panel that was a worse version of both.

## Outcome

- **The sale completes at the counter.** A merchant can extend Kredim credit
  directly to their own customer, with no one from Kredim in the loop of a
  transaction that belongs to the merchant.
- **Visibility follows the organisation chart.** Branch scoping means a store
  sees its own trade and a head office sees across its branches, without either
  view being assembled by hand.
- **Credit logic exists once.** Merchant and the Member Panel share it, so a
  change to how credit behaves lands in both without being written twice.
- **The narrow surface is the point.** The panel does one thing at counter
  speed rather than being an administrative console that store staff have to
  learn.
